The Data Commissioner awards damages routinely. A judge has just asked whether it can.

A High Court appeal has set aside a compensation award and flagged something larger than the figure. The power the Office of the Data Protection Commissioner uses to put a shilling value on a privacy breach may not sit in the Act at all. That is not a technicality. It is an invitation.

MO Mark G. Omuga Partner, Omuma Advocates LLP

Commentary is independent analysis and argument by the named author. It is written to prompt discussion, not to state the firm’s advice or a settled position, and it is not a substitute for advice on your own matter.

Every week the Office of the Data Protection Commissioner receives a complaint, investigates it, finds a breach, and orders the offending business to pay the complainant a sum of money. Controllers treat that last step as settled furniture. It is not. A High Court judge, hearing an appeal from one such award, has just said in terms that the power to make it may not exist, and has invited someone to come and test the point properly.

The facts of the appeal were grim and are not the reason it matters. A patient was recorded by a CCTV camera in an examination room during an intimate procedure. She complained, the Commissioner investigated, found against the clinic, ordered it to overhaul its data practices and to pay her over a million shillings, and the clinic appealed. The appeal succeeded on the money and failed on everything else. The judge upheld the order to fix the clinic’s practices, which is the part that actually costs a business, and set aside the award of compensation. That split is where the interest lies.

What the Act gives, and what it leaves out

The Data Protection Act arms the Commissioner carefully. It spells out powers to investigate, to issue enforcement notices, to impose penalties and administrative fines. Each of those is conferred in express words. When the Act turns to compensation, it says only that a person who suffers damage from a breach is entitled to it from the controller. It does not say who decides the claim, or how, or on what measure. The provision creating the entitlement sits after the provision giving the right of appeal to the High Court, a small structural tell that compensation was meant to be a matter for a court rather than a step in the regulator’s own process.

The power the Commissioner actually uses comes from somewhere else. It comes from a regulation, which lists an order for compensation among the remedies the Commissioner may grant at the end of an investigation. The judge’s point was simple and, to my mind, hard to answer on the face of the statute. A regulation cannot confer a power the parent Act withheld. If the Act does not give the Commissioner the power to award damages, a regulation made under it cannot manufacture one.

The question the earlier cases skipped

The obvious objection is that this ship has sailed. The High Court has already upheld the Commissioner’s power to award compensation, more than once, and a single judge cannot unsettle that on his own. Look closely at what those earlier courts were asked, though, because it is not the same question.

In one such appeal, the court upheld a compensation award and held it was within the Commissioner’s jurisdiction. It reached that by reading the regulation and taking it at face value. It was not asked whether the regulation was valid in the first place, so it did not decide that.

In a separate constitutional challenge, a litigant argued the opposite extreme, that giving the Commissioner power to award remedies at all was unconstitutional because it handed a regulator the work of a court. The High Court rejected that too, holding that the Commissioner’s functions are administrative and regulatory rather than judicial, and that a specialised body resolving complaints is the kind of mechanism the Constitution encourages. That decision answers whether the Constitution permits Parliament to give the Commissioner this power. It does not answer whether Parliament, in this Act, actually did.

That gap is the whole point. One case assumed the power and measured the award. The other asked whether conferring the power would be constitutional and said yes. Neither asked the plain statutory question the latest judge asked, which is whether the Act conferred the power at all. A doubt that looks like one judge breaking ranks is really one judge noticing a question the earlier cases walked past.

So the honest position is narrow and precise. It is not that the Commissioner clearly cannot award damages, because two courts have treated the power as real. It is that the one argument capable of removing it has never been put to a court and decided, and a judge who looked at it thought it had force.

Why a controller should care now

For a business, that narrowness is the useful part. The assumption that these awards are beyond challenge is worth testing, not because the challenge is sure to succeed, it is novel and runs against the grain of two decisions, but because it has never been run and a High Court judge has now flagged it as arguable. A business that has paid an ODPC compensation award, or is facing one, has a point worth preserving. It will not touch the enforcement obligations, the clean-up, the signage, the training, the impact assessments, which stand on firm statutory ground. It goes only to the money, which is usually the part that stings.

None of this is a reason to ignore the Commissioner or to treat a complaint lightly. The regulator’s core powers are real and the penalties for defying an enforcement notice are serious. It is a reason to stop treating one specific remedy as settled, and to reserve the point rather than pay as if it were beyond question.

The latest judge did not resolve it, and was right not to try to on an appeal. He pointed to where it has to be resolved, in a proper challenge with full argument and the Attorney General in the room. Whoever brings that challenge first will shape the answer for everyone. The question is on the record now. It is only a matter of who puts it.
