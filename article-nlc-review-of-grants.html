When public land became private: the title the NLC can reopen

The National Land Commission's power to reopen public land titles is back, a recent High Court judgment means it cannot simply be run out by time, and a Supreme Court decision has made the protection the law offers an innocent buyer thinner than it looks. The safety of a title now turns on its root, not its face.

The National Land Commission has recovered a power that unsettles a great deal of Kenyan land. From 4 November 2025, under the National Land Commission (Amendment) Act, it can once again review a grant or disposition of public land made before the 2010 Constitution, decide whether it was lawful, and act on what it finds. Thousands of files are waiting, held up only until the procedural regulations are gazetted. For anyone who holds land that began life as a public allocation, which is a large part of urban and peri-urban Kenya, the question is not the backlog but how safe the title really is once the Commission, or a court, is entitled to look behind it.

A power with teeth, not just recommendations

The reopened review runs on the Commission's own motion or on a complaint, and it draws a line the earlier regime did not. Where a title was acquired unlawfully, the Commission, after hearing the parties, directs the Registrar to revoke it. Where the acquisition was merely irregular, it takes steps to correct the irregularity and may make consequential orders, rather than cancel. The sharper change is enforcement. The Commission's determination is no longer a recommendation that someone else may act on or ignore. It can be registered in the High Court and enforced as an order of the court. That turns the review from an advisory exercise into something that can move a title on its own force.

A clock the Constitution may not allow

The Act puts a five-year period on the exercise, and whether that limit can stand is already in doubt. In a petition brought by Okiya Omtatah, the High Court held in November 2025 that reviewing grants and redressing historical land injustices are continuing constitutional functions under Articles 67 and 68, and that Parliament cannot place a timeline on them, striking down the five-year caps as unconstitutional. Read with that judgment, the review power is effectively open-ended. A title traceable to a pre-2010 public allocation is not made safe by the passage of time, and the comfort that the window will simply close is misplaced.

The shield for the innocent buyer, and its limits

The Act offers one reassurance that owners will reach for. No revocation may be effected against a bona fide purchaser for value without notice of a defect in the title. On its face that protects the innocent buyer who paid a fair price and knew of nothing wrong. The difficulty is that the Supreme Court has already shown how little that doctrine does where the root of a title is bad. In Dina Management Limited v County Government of Mombasa the court held that a title is the end product of a process, that an enquiry into a challenged title must go to the root, right from the first allotment, and that indefeasibility cannot sanction an illegal or irregular allocation of public land. On the facts the land was beachfront public land whose first allocation was defective, and the current owner, though it had bought and paid, could not shelter behind the bona fide purchaser doctrine, because a seller with no valid root had nothing good to pass on. Article 40(6) put it beyond argument, since the Constitution withholds its protection from property found to have been unlawfully acquired.

So the statutory shield protects the buyer of a genuinely clean title and does much less for the buyer whose land traces back to a tainted first grant, which is precisely the land the Commission's review is aimed at. The protection in the Act and the power in the Act are pointed at different titles.

The burden sits with the person holding the title

The same judgment carries the practical lesson. Where the root of a title is challenged, it is not enough to produce the certificate; the owner must prove that the acquisition was lawful. The burden of establishing a good title rests on the person holding it, and the Supreme Court said a buyer of land with an obvious public character ought to have been more cautious before parting with money. The answer, for a buyer, a lender taking land as security, or a developer assembling a site, is due diligence that reaches past the clean face of the register to the origin of the title, how the land first came out of public hands, and whether that first step was lawful. A title whose root cannot be defended is exposed whatever the register says, and the review now gives that exposure a forum.

None of this makes every title derived from public land unsafe. Most grants were lawful and will survive scrutiny. What has moved is the question. The clean face of the register is no longer the end of the inquiry, the lawfulness of the first allocation is, and that is a reason to check the root before buying, lending or building, rather than after a notice from the Commission arrives.
