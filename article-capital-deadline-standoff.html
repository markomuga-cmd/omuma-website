CBK says 2029, the Treasury says 2032: which bank capital deadline binds

The Central Bank has told banks to ignore the Treasury's move to push the Sh10 billion core capital deadline out to 2032 and to keep to the staggered climb that ends in 2029. For a bank caught in the middle, the only question that matters is which deadline is law, and on the record so far that is the Central Bank's.

Kenya's banks are watching their regulator and their ministry take opposite positions on the same rule, and the gap between them is real money. The Central Bank of Kenya has directed banks to ignore the National Treasury's decision to relax the timetable for raising core capital, and to keep building toward the figures the law already sets. For a bank sitting below the mark, knowing which side is right is not academic.

Start with the law as it stands. The Business Laws (Amendment) Act, 2024 raised the minimum core capital a bank must hold from 1 billion shillings to 10 billion, to be reached by the end of 2029, built up in yearly steps. The step that matters now falls at the end of 2026, when a bank must hold at least 5 billion. The Central Bank has enforced this without softening. Its governor has said there is no grace period, and earlier this year around fifteen banks still needed to find a combined 33.6 billion shillings between them to clear the 2026 bar. Roughly a dozen banks remain short of the eventual 10 billion.

The Treasury blinked, the regulator did not

Then came the Treasury's move. The Cabinet Secretary agreed to lenders' request to drop the annual milestones and replace them with a single hard deadline of December 2032, on the argument that the yearly targets were squeezing lending as smaller banks rushed to comply. On the reporting, that announcement landed a day after the Central Bank had said the deadlines were not moving. The front page this week is the regulator's reply: banks should ignore the Cabinet Secretary's statement and stay on the staggered path.

So which deadline binds

This is where the law, rather than the louder voice, decides. The minimum core capital a bank must hold is fixed by statute, in the Banking Act as amended by the 2024 Act. A statute is changed by Parliament, or by a power the statute itself hands to the minister and that the minister validly uses, not by a public statement of intent. On the record so far, no amendment and no legal notice giving the 2032 date force has appeared. Until one does, the schedule in the 2024 Act is the law, and the Central Bank, as the body that administers that Act, is enforcing it. That is why it can tell banks to treat the Cabinet Secretary's announcement as having no effect on what they must do.

What a bank should actually do

The practical course follows from that. The safe assumption is the statutory one. A bank that eases off on the strength of a 2032 date that has not been enacted is betting that the extension will become law before the regulator acts on the shortfall, and the Central Bank has shown it is willing to fine banks that miss capital rules. Keep building capital on the timetable the Act sets, through retained earnings, a rights issue, fresh investment or a merger, and treat the 2032 date as a possibility to plan around rather than a deadline to rely on. The one thing worth watching is the statute book. If an amendment or a gazette notice gives the 2032 deadline legal effect, the position changes overnight, and the banks that tracked it will have moved first.

Until then, when the regulator and the ministry disagree, the deadline with legal force is the one written into the Act.
