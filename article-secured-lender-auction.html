A valid charge is not enough: when a botched auction makes the lender the debtor

The Court of Appeal has confirmed that a bank holding a perfectly good charge can still be ordered to pay its defaulting borrower the full value of the security, if it cannot prove it served the statutory notice and sold the property at a fair price. Development Bank of Kenya v Angwenyi is a hard lesson for any lender realising security.

In Development Bank of Kenya Ltd v Angwenyi, decided on 16 October 2026, the Court of Appeal left standing an award that turned a secured lender into a judgment debtor. The borrower had defaulted. His charge was valid. And the bank still walked away owing him money.

The facts are ordinary enough. In 1998 the borrower took overdraft facilities from the bank, secured by a charge over land at Thika and a pledge of bank shares. He fell into default. The bank’s own valuer put the open market value of the land at 2 million shillings and its forced sale value at 1.2 million. The first auction failed. The property was later sold at a public auction for 650,000 shillings, little more than half the forced sale value. The borrower sued, and both the High Court and the Court of Appeal found for him.

The bank lost on process, not on its security

What is striking is where the bank lost. Not on its security. The court confirmed that the 1998 letter of offer created a valid charge and a valid pledge of shares, and it rejected the borrower’s late argument that no security had been perfected. The paperwork was sound. The bank lost on process.

The first and fatal failure was the statutory notice. Under section 69A of the Indian Transfer of Property Act, the law then governing the sale, a chargee cannot exercise its power of sale until it has served a notice demanding payment and the borrower has stayed in default for three months. The bank produced a notice. It could not prove it had served it. Once the borrower denied receiving the notice, the burden sat with the bank to show service, and the bank had no certificate of posting to produce. One of the bank’s own officers admitted that notices had been drawn but not sent. Without proof of service, the court held, the sale was not merely irregular. It was a nullity.

The second failure was price. An auction is not a licence to dump the asset. A valuation had been done within the twelve months the Auctioneers Rules require, so the bank knew the forced sale value was 1.2 million. Selling at 650,000, with no fresh search, no explanation for the low bid and no evidence of any real effort to attract better offers, was a sale at an undervalue. The Court of Appeal, following its own decision in Euro Bank Limited (In Liquidation) v Twictor Investments, repeated that a chargee must prove it took all reasonable steps to sell at a fair price.

Why the bill was so heavy

Then came the sting. The measure of damages depends on what went wrong. Where a sale is merely irregular, the borrower recovers the shortfall, the gap between a fair price and the price achieved. But where the sale is a nullity, the borrower is entitled to the full market value of the property at the time of sale. So the bank was ordered to pay 2 million shillings, the open market value, with interest, and costs. A lender that was owed money ended the case writing a cheque to the borrower.

The bank tried to soften the blow by asking that the award be set off against the debt the borrower still owed. It could not. The court held that set-off must be specifically pleaded, and the bank’s defence had merely alleged that a balance remained outstanding. It had pleaded no set-off and no counterclaim. Having failed to plead it, the bank could not net the two figures against each other, and the full award stood. That is the quiet lesson in the case, and the most avoidable one.

The Court of Appeal dismissed both the bank’s appeal and the borrower’s cross-appeal, and left each side to carry its own costs.

The same discipline under the Land Act

The case was decided under statutes now repealed, the Indian Transfer of Property Act and the Registration of Titles Act, but the discipline it enforces has not changed. The Land Act 2012 carries the same two duties, a proper statutory notice before any sale and a sale at the best price reasonably obtainable, and the courts read them just as strictly. For any lender, the practical points are plain. Keep proof that every statutory notice was served, not just sent. Obtain a current valuation and hold the sale to it. Record why a bid was accepted, especially a low one. And if you intend to chase the borrower for the balance after a sale, plead the set-off or the counterclaim, because the right to net off is lost if it is not raised.

A charge is only as good as the process used to enforce it.
