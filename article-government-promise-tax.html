A government promise is not a tax exemption

A contractor built part of the Nairobi Expressway on the Treasury’s promise to carry the tax, and was told to pay it anyway. A factory imported its plant under an exemption the taxman itself issued, and kept it. Two Kenyan decisions, read together, draw a precise line: a promise by the government is not a tax exemption, except where the taxman itself lawfully made it.

A contractor helped build the Nairobi Expressway on an undertaking from the National Treasury that the government would carry the tax on the materials it consumed. A roofing-sheet maker imported the plant for an eleven-billion-shilling factory under a VAT exemption the taxman itself had issued and revalidated three times. When the bill came in each case, the government’s promise was tested, and the two came out opposite ways. Read together, they give a precise answer to a question deal-makers underestimate.

Only the law can create an exemption

Begin with the rule above everything. Under Article 210 of the Constitution, no tax may be imposed, waived or varied except as provided by legislation. A tax is a creature of statute, and so is any relief from it. A Cabinet Secretary cannot create an exemption by letter, the Treasury cannot create one by agreeing to foot the bill, and a side arrangement between a ministry, a company and the taxman cannot do what only an Act of Parliament can.

When the promise fails

That rule decided the expressway case. In Cale Infrastructure Construction Company Limited v Commissioner of Customs and Border Control, the contractor faced a demand of about Sh5.7 billion on materials consumed in the project, even though the National Treasury had undertaken to bear that very liability. The High Court held the taxpayer liable. The legal duty to pay the tax rests on the importer under the charging statutes, and the Treasury’s letter of undertaking, the court held, did not have the legal effect of transferring that statutory liability or of obliging the revenue authority to chase the Treasury in the taxpayer’s place. The taxman was not a party to the arrangement, and on the government’s default it could recover from the person the law makes liable. It did not even matter that everyone agreed the tax was due; the only question was who had to pay it, and the answer was the taxpayer. A promise that the State will settle your tax is only as good as the State’s willingness to settle it.

When the taxman’s own word binds

The roofing case went the other way, and the difference is the whole lesson. In Maisha Mabati Mills Limited v Commissioner, the company had imported plant for a new factory at Lukenya under a VAT exemption that the Commissioner for Customs had put in writing, operationalised through a system exemption code, and then revalidated three times over nearly three years. The company cleared its machinery under that exemption, built its financing around it, and gave up the input-VAT claim it would otherwise have made. Five years after the imports, the taxman reversed course and demanded about Sh1.44 billion, swollen by penalties and interest. The Tax Appeals Tribunal set the demand aside. It did not disturb Cale. It upheld it, and accepted that a bare Treasury undertaking cannot exempt anyone. But it held this case apart on a single fact: in Cale the revenue had never issued an exemption at all, while here the revenue itself had granted one, operated it, and confirmed it again and again. Where the representation comes from the taxman’s own hand, the taxman is bound by it.

The principle the tribunal applied is not new, and it comes from the top. The Supreme Court held in Kenya Revenue Authority v Export Trading Company Ltd that once the revenue makes a representation to a taxpayer in the discharge of its functions it is bound by it, and cannot sit on its hands, under-collect through its own system, and then years later demand the difference as though its own error were the taxpayer’s problem. That is legitimate expectation, and it does real work in tax, where it is too often waved away. The same principle carried a customs case we covered recently, where a revenue body that vacated a demand and promised a change would be prospective could not later pretend the promise was never made.

The limit that reconciles the two

There is a limit, and it is the limit that reconciles the two outcomes with Article 210. A legitimate expectation cannot be built on an illegality. The taxman is held to a promise it had the power to make, not to one it did not, so the real question in the roofing case was whether the exemption was lawful in the first place. The tribunal found that it was. The relief had a statutory footing, in the VAT Act’s exemption for capital goods brought in to expand manufacturing, which Parliament had made available for investments of at least two billion shillings, and in the Public Finance Management Act’s provision allowing the Cabinet Secretary to waive a national tax where an Act of Parliament authorises it. Because the exemption rested on a power the law actually confers, the expectation it created was lawful, and it bound the Commissioner. Had the exemption been nothing more than administrative effect given to a Treasury promise, as in Cale, Article 210 would have sunk it. The line between the two cases is the line between a benefit the authority lawfully conferred and a promise nobody had the power to make.

Two cautions

Two cautions keep the lesson honest. The expressway case is a decision of the High Court; the roofing case is a decision of the Tax Appeals Tribunal, which sits below it, and the revenue may yet appeal, so the last word on these facts may not have been spoken. And the result is fact-sensitive: take away the statutory basis for the exemption, or the taxman’s own repeated confirmations, and the taxpayer is back in Cale.

The lawful way to let a tax go

It is worth remembering that the law does provide a clean way to let a tax go, and it looks nothing like a side letter. Section 37F of the Tax Procedures Act lets the Commissioner refrain from recovering a tax on defined grounds, but only with the Cabinet Secretary’s prior written approval, and only if the write-off is gazetted, with the taxpayer named and the amount stated, and laid before Parliament, which can annul it. Relief that is meant to be given is given in the open, with a reason recorded and a signature at the top, not buried in a drawer.

The practical reading

For any business whose dealings with the government turn on tax relief, the lessons are concrete. A promise that the State will pay or forgive your tax binds no one unless it is grounded in a power the law actually gives, so insist that the relief sits on a statute, or on a lawful and properly issued approval from the authority that administers the tax, and keep the paper, because years later the fight turns on exactly what was represented and by whom. Where the taxman has itself granted and operated an exemption, it can be held to it. Where all you hold is someone else’s undertaking to pay, you hold a credit risk on the promisor, and the statute still has your name on it.

This is general information, not legal advice. If your dealings with the government turn on a tax exemption, a waiver or an undertaking, speak to us about the legal basis of the relief you are relying on before you commit to it.
