A trust in a drawer no longer counts. Kenya's new trust law, and the two-year clock.

Every written trust in Kenya must now be registered or incorporated, or a court will not enforce it. The new Trust Administration Act sets up a Registrar of Trusts, pulls trusts into the beneficial ownership regime, and gives existing trusts until September 2028 to comply. For family and commercial structures alike, registration is now the thing that counts.

The Trust Administration Act, 2026 commenced on 25 September 2026. It consolidates how trusts are formed, registered and run, and it repeals the two statutes that governed Kenyan trusts for decades, the Trustees (Perpetual Succession) Act and the Trustees Act. For anyone who holds family or business assets through a trust, it changes the one thing that used to be optional: registration.

Registration is now the whole game

A trust can still be created in writing or implied by conduct. But a written trust must now be registered or incorporated with a new Registrar of Trusts, an office set up within the Business Registration Service (section 77). Until it is, the trust is not enforceable (section 5), and a trust that has not been registered or incorporated can be declared invalid (section 6). There is a safety valve: a person claiming under an unregistered written trust can still ask the court to recognise or enforce it (section 5(4)). But the settlor's own paperwork no longer does the work on its own. A deed sitting unregistered in a drawer is no longer something you can simply rely on.

Registered or incorporated, and why it matters

These are two different things. A registered trust stays unincorporated and acquires no legal personality; its certificate proves it exists, nothing more (section 27). An incorporated trust becomes a body corporate with perpetual succession, able to own property and sue in its own name (section 32). A registered trust can convert to an incorporated one later (section 33). For most family structures the choice is practical: incorporation gives the trust a legal identity that survives changes of trustee and holds title in its own name.

Beneficial ownership, and who can see it

The Act pulls trusts fully into the beneficial ownership regime. Every trust must keep a register of its beneficial owners and lodge a copy with the Registrar (sections 65 and 66). Changes are filed within twenty-one days (section 67), records kept for seven years (section 68), and the information is open to competent authorities, which the Act defines to include the Attorney-General, criminal investigation agencies, the Central Bank, the Financial Reporting Centre and the Kenya Revenue Authority (section 2). A trust is no longer a private arrangement that sits outside the view of the state.

Duties you cannot draft away

Trustee duties are now statutory, and a trust deed can no longer sign them away. A deed cannot exclude a trustee's liability for dishonesty, wilful misconduct or gross negligence, and that applies to trusts created before as well as after the Act (section 62). The penalties have weight. A breach of trust carries an administrative penalty of up to one million shillings for an individual and five million for a company (section 61). Disposing of trust property so as to defeat the trust is an offence punishable by a fine of up to five million shillings, or twenty million for a company, and imprisonment of up to five years (section 46). A trustee who ignores a Registrar's directive can be disqualified for up to three years (section 69).

Family trusts, and why the tax code rewards registering

Family trusts have their own footing. A family trust can be living or testamentary, set up to hold and preserve family wealth across generations, and it must be a non-trading entity (section 10). It needs only one trustee, where charitable and purpose trusts need three individuals or a corporate trustee (section 11).

The Trust Administration Act does not itself deal with tax, but the tax code rewards putting a family trust on a proper footing. Under the Income Tax Act, the principal sum of a registered family trust is exempt from income tax (First Schedule, paragraph 57), and the capital gains that would otherwise arise on moving immovable property or shares into the trust are not charged (First Schedule, paragraphs 36 and 58). Transfers of property into a registered family trust are also exempt from stamp duty (sections 52 and 117 of the Stamp Duty Act). Each of those reliefs turns on the trust being registered, which is now a matter for this Act. Registration is the gateway to both enforceability and the tax treatment.

The two-year clock

The deadline is the part to diarise. Existing trusts have twenty-four months from commencement, to 25 September 2028, to comply (section 99). Trusts already incorporated under the old law are treated as continuing under the new Act, but they too must lodge their beneficial ownership register within that window (section 66). Annual returns are now due within thirty days of the trust's registration anniversary (section 75).

The practical message is the same for a family holding its home and investments through a trust and for a company holding assets through one. Find the deed, check whether the structure is registered, and start the clock from there.

This is general information, not legal advice. If you hold assets through a trust, or you are weighing whether to set one up, speak to us about registration or incorporation and the September 2028 deadline before it runs down, not after.
