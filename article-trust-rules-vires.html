A power the Act did not give: the vires question in the draft trust rules

The draft Trust Administration Regulations 2026 would let a trustee suspend a beneficiary’s distributions where the beneficiary will not disclose who they are. The sharper question is whether a regulation can lawfully create a sanction the Act itself withheld. We read the draft against its parent Act, and against the limits on what a regulation can do.

The draft Trust Administration Regulations 2026 would let a trustee suspend a beneficiary’s distributions where the beneficiary will not disclose who they are. Before asking how that power works, a trustee should ask a prior question: whether a regulation can lawfully create it at all.

A regulation cannot outrun its Act

Regulations are subsidiary legislation. They draw their force from the Act that authorises them and cannot travel beyond it. Here the authority is section 96 of the Trust Administration Act, 2026, which lets the Attorney-General make regulations "not inconsistent with" the Act and "necessary to give effect to" it, and then lists what they may prescribe: beneficial ownership information, the manner and form of lodgment, fees, the requirements for corporate trustees, the disqualification of trustees, and like matters. The list is about information, process and the conduct of trustees. Nothing in it speaks to restricting or withholding a beneficiary’s benefit.

The Act aimed its enforcement at the trustee

The Act’s own enforcement points the same way. Part IX makes the trustee the target. A trust must keep a register of its beneficial owners and lodge it, and a trustee who fails faces an administrative penalty and, under section 69, a Registrar’s directive and ultimately disqualification for up to three years. That is the sanction the Act chose for non-disclosure, and it runs against the trustee, not the beneficiary. The Act nowhere reaches into a beneficiary’s entitlement.

A sanction the Act withheld

Against that scheme, the draft’s restriction power is a new sanction, aimed at a target the Act did not sanction, touching an economic interest the Act left alone. A regulation that supplies a penalty the parent Act withheld is the classic case of delegated legislation outrunning its source, and it is open to challenge as beyond the enabling power and inconsistent with the Act.

Arguable, which is why it should be raised now

The point is arguable, not settled, which is exactly why it should be made now. The contrary case is that section 96 is expressed without prejudice to the generality of the main power, that a restriction can be cast as nothing more than a means of compelling the disclosure the Act does require, and that the draft’s own limits, proportionality, no extinguishing of the interest, subject to the deed and to any court order, are meant to keep it in bounds. A court could accept that. It could also find that compelling disclosure by freezing distributions is a substantive step the Act reserved to itself and did not delegate. The uncertainty is the problem, and it is cheaper to resolve in the consultation than in litigation.

Weakest where it would matter most

The uncertainty has a practical edge, because the power is weakest where it would matter most. Those same guardrails bite hardest on a beneficiary with a vested or fixed entitlement, the interest the draft is most careful not to disturb, and barely at all on a discretionary beneficiary, who had no right to a distribution to begin with. A trustee who relies on a shaky regulation to freeze a vested share may find it had no power to do so, and a breach-of-trust claim waiting. Beneath that sits a quieter exposure: the identity and address data the draft requires is sensitive personal data, and the trust that holds and lodges it answers for it under the Data Protection Act.

The regulations are still a draft, and the Business Registration Service is taking written comments up to 20 November 2026. The vires of the restriction power is the first thing worth putting in a memorandum. We are reviewing the draft for clients and can make the submission or advise on how to structure and run a trust while the position settles.
