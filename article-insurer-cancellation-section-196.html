When an insurer loses its licence, what happens to your cover

When the Insurance Regulatory Authority cancels an insurer’s registration, policyholders and claimants are left with one urgent question: what happens to my cover and my claim. The Insurance Act has a clear answer, and it is at once more reassuring, and more limited, than most people expect.

The law does not cancel an insurer lightly, and the usual trigger is money. An insurer must maintain a capital adequacy ratio of one hundred per cent at all times, a measure of whether it holds enough capital against the risks on its books. An insurer that falls below that line is, by the Act itself, deemed unable to pay its debts, which is the same test that opens the door to winding up. The Act adds a second trigger of the same kind, that an insurer which fails to pay a claim is also deemed unable to pay its debts and liable to be wound up. So an insurer in capital distress, or one that has started turning claims away, has already tripped the statutory wire long before a cancellation arrives.

Cancellation itself runs through section 196 of the Insurance Act. The Commissioner of Insurance may cancel a registration, but only after giving the insurer a reasonable opportunity to make representations, and only on defined grounds, among them failing to comply with the Act and not being conducted on sound insurance principles. The cancellation does not bite at once. It takes effect thirty days after the notice, a window that matters more than it first appears.

Cancelled, but not erased

Here is the part policyholders most need to understand. A cancellation stops the insurer taking on new business, but it does not tear up the cover already in force. The Act says that once a registration is cancelled the insurer may write no new contract of insurance, while all rights and liabilities under contracts entered into before the cancellation continue as if the cancellation had not taken place. Your policy, and a claim you have already lodged, survive the cancellation. They must still be honoured, and the business of paying them runs off rather than stopping dead. What you lose is the future, because you cannot renew with that insurer, so you will need to arrange fresh cover elsewhere before your policy runs out.

Cancellation, or the lighter tool

Cancellation is also not the Authority’s only tool, nor usually its first. The Act gives the regulator a lighter, rehabilitative option in section 67C, the power to place an insurer under statutory management. There the Commissioner, with the approval of his board, appoints a manager who takes over the running of the insurer from its directors for up to twelve months, and whose job is to work out whether the business can be revived or ought to be liquidated. It is the tool for a situation that still looks fixable, or at least one that can be wound down in an orderly and supervised way while there is value to protect, and the regulator reaches for it far more readily than it cancels a licence outright.

So why would the regulator go straight to cancellation. It takes the terminal step when rehabilitation is not realistic and the softer route has already been exhausted, because statutory management does not cure the usual problem. A capital shortfall is a hole in the balance sheet, and a manager can stabilise and ration but cannot fill it, so where the owners will not put in new capital, management only prolongs the loss and leaves the Compensation Fund exposed to the running of a hollow book. The two tools often meet in any event, because statutory management itself tends to end in liquidation, the manager’s report being the very document that recommends reviving or winding up the insurer. There is a twist in this that matters to a claimant, and it cuts against the labels. A cancellation keeps existing claims alive, since they continue as if it had not happened, whereas a statutory manager may declare a moratorium that freezes payments to policyholders and creditors across the board, and even stops the limitation clock while it runs. Which regime leaves a claimant better off turns less on the name than on whether there is money behind the policy, but the mechanics differ, and it is worth knowing which one you are in.

Because statutory management exists, its very availability is the argument an insurer runs to attack a cancellation. The complaint is that the Authority jumped to the drastic option without trying the lighter one, so that the cancellation was premature or disproportionate. That is a genuine ground of challenge, and it is fought on the section 196 requirement that the insurer first be given a reasonable opportunity to make representations. Whether a regulator was entitled to skip statutory management is, in a contested case, a question for the court.

The floor, and its ceiling

The survival of your claim on paper is not the same thing as being paid in full. A cancellation usually rides on the very distress the capital rule is built to catch, so the real question becomes whether the insurer can meet what it owes. This is where the Policyholders Compensation Fund comes in. The Fund compensates policyholders and claimants of an insurer that is placed under statutory management or loses its licence, but only up to two hundred and fifty thousand shillings for each claim. A proposal to double that ceiling to five hundred thousand shillings has been floated, but it is not yet law. Above the cap you stand as an ordinary creditor in the wind-up and recover whatever the liquidation eventually pays. For a modest motor or medical claim the Fund may cover the whole of it; for a large one it is a floor, not a full answer.

The people in the last thirty days

The Act also puts a leash on the people running the insurer during that thirty-day window. The directors and the principal officer are personally liable for any act or omission in the interim period before the cancellation takes effect. That is a deliberate guard against the temptation to strip assets, prefer favoured creditors, or write business that ought not to be written while the clock runs down. For anyone sitting on the board of an insurer in that position, it is a reason to do nothing that could not survive daylight, and to document every decision taken in the meantime.

What to do

So what should you do if your insurer is cancelled. If you hold a policy, do not treat the cancellation as the end of your cover, because it continues, but arrange a replacement before it lapses and keep your premium and claim records in order. If you have a pending claim, pursue it, because it survives, but understand the compensation cap and the recovery risk, and find out who now stands in the insurer’s shoes, since a statutory manager or a liquidator usually takes over the handling. And weigh the value of concluding a claim sooner rather than later, because a sum agreed and paid while there is still money behind it is worth more than a judgment enforced against an empty shell. The law, in short, does not abandon you when an insurer goes down. It keeps your contract alive, it gives you a guaranteed floor through the Fund, and it holds the insurer’s leaders personally to account in the final days. What it cannot do is promise that a failed insurer will pay every shilling it owes.
