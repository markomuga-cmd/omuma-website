Why the excise stamp-fee judgment settled nothing

The High Court sent the Law Society's challenge back for coming too early. Whether the higher excise stamp fees are lawful is still an open question.

Last month's High Court decision on excise stamp fees was reported as a defeat for the Law Society of Kenya and a win for the taxman. The judgment is more modest than that, and more interesting. In Law Society of Kenya v National Assembly & 3 others [2026] KEHC 16663 (KLR), delivered on 23 October 2026, the court did not uphold the higher fees, and it did not reject the Society's arguments against them. It struck the petition out as premature. The constitutional questions the case raised are still waiting to be answered.

The charge in dispute is the fee manufacturers pay for the security stamps fixed to excisable goods under the Kenya Revenue Authority's Excisable Goods Management System. Legal Notice No. 30 of 2023 raised those per-stamp fees sharply. On the figures circulated during the consultation, the stamp on a bottle of beer roughly doubled and the stamp on cosmetics rose several times over. Manufacturers pay for every stamp issued, so the increase falls on brewers, distillers, soft-drink makers and cosmetics firms in direct proportion to what they produce.

The court answered one question, and only one

The whole case turned on a single issue: had the Society come to court too soon? The court held that it had. Regulations of this kind must first pass through the National Assembly's Committee on Delegated Legislation, which scrutinises them under the Statutory Instruments Act for public participation, cost and constitutionality before a court is asked to weigh in. Treating that committee as the first port of call, the judge found no proof that its report had been tabled or adopted, concluded that the legislative process was still running, and declined to intervene while Parliament had not yet had its say.

Because the case was decided on that threshold, the merits were left untouched. The Society's three substantive complaints, that the regulations were made without meaningful public participation, without the regulatory impact assessment the law requires, and in breach of the Constitution's taxation principles, were all set aside unheard. A strike-out for coming early is not a finding that the fees are sound.

The argument that is still alive

The sharpest of those complaints is the one worth watching. The Authority's own evidence explained that the revised fees were expected to raise enough to meet the cost of the stamps and to clear an outstanding debt of KSh 4.5 billion owed to the stamp supplier. A fee is meant to recover the cost of a service or of regulation. A charge calibrated to pay down a debt starts to look like something else.

In the court's own record, the Authority said the revised fees were expected to generate sufficient revenue to meet the cost of excise stamps and offset the outstanding debt of KSh 4.5 billion. For a lawyer, that single sentence is the most interesting line in the file. If a future court reaches the merits, it will have to decide whether a stamp fee set partly to retire a liability has crossed the line from a fee into a tax. That matters, because a tax pulls the charge into Article 210 of the Constitution and the full framework the law reserves for taxation, where a charge cannot be imposed or varied except as legislation provides. That case has not been argued yet. It is the one the Society will build for its return.

Not the first round, and not the last

The stamps have been in court before. The original 2017 system, made under the Excise Duty Act, 2015, was struck down for inadequate public participation, and the 2023 increase has been held back by court orders for most of three years as the dispute moved through the High Court and the Court of Appeal. The 23 October judgment is the latest chapter in a long fight, not the end of it.

For manufacturers and their advisers, the practical position has shifted less than the headlines suggest. The fees have not been blessed, and the strongest argument against them remains open. The prematurity holding is itself open to challenge, since the regulations were already gazetted and being enforced, and scrutiny by Parliament and review by the courts are different functions that can run alongside each other rather than one waiting on the other.

What will decide where this goes next

Three things will shape what happens next. The first is whether the Law Society appeals the strike-out, or re-files once Parliament concludes its scrutiny. The second is whether the Committee on Delegated Legislation has in fact reported on the regulations, which would clear the prematurity obstacle altogether. The third is what the gazetted fee schedule actually says when read against the figures that have circulated since the consultation. We are watching all three.
