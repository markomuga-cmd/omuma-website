The taxman's word: why a customs demand could not come back from the dead

The Court of Appeal has struck down a Sh45 million customs demand because the Commissioner had agreed to drop it and promised that any later change of tariff would touch only future imports. The judgment is a clean map of three ways a revenue authority can lose a classification fight: a promise it went back on, a reclassification it tried to reach backwards, and evidence it slipped in on appeal.

The product at the centre of this case is a reminder that a tariff heading can be worth millions. Twiga Chemical Industries imported prilled porous ammonium nitrate, the low-density, absorbent grade of ammonium nitrate that is the base material for ANFO blasting explosives rather than the ordinary fertiliser prill. For years the Commissioner had treated ammonium nitrate as a duty-free fertiliser input under tariff heading 3102.30.00. Then, in October 2008, he reclassified this particular product under heading 3602.00.00, the heading for prepared explosives, which carries duty, and raised a demand of about Sh45.5 million in duty and VAT. That single move, from the fertiliser chapter to the explosives chapter, is the whole dispute.

What happened next is what made the demand collapse. Twiga objected, the parties met, and on 27 March 2009 the Commissioner wrote a letter that did three things in plain words. It said the product had the same composition as the ammonium nitrate on which the earlier, duty-free ruling had been made. It said the Commissioner was willing to vacate the demand on the product. And it said that should there be any change in tariff, after a second opinion the Commissioner had sought from the World Customs Organization, the change would be communicated to Twiga for use in future importations. The World Customs Organization gave its opinion in 2011, siding with the explosives heading, and the Commissioner then revived the old demand for the imports made in the intervening years. The Tax Appeals Tribunal and the High Court both sided with the Commissioner. The Court of Appeal did not.

A revenue authority can be held to its word

The first and deepest holding is that a revenue authority can be held to its word. The court read the 27 March 2009 letter the way it read, as a decision to vacate the demand, to put the product back in the duty-free class, and to apply any future change only to imports made after the change. Having made that representation and having invited Twiga to rely on it, the Commissioner could not later treat it as if it had never been written. This is the doctrine of legitimate expectation doing real work in a tax setting, where it is too often waved away. The Commissioner's answer was the familiar one, that a legitimate expectation cannot arise out of an illegality, so a promise not to tax cannot bind the State. The court met it squarely. The Commissioner had not promised to break the law. He had done something squarely within his power, reinstated a tariff classification that attracted no duty and undertaken to apply any change prospectively, and a promise made within the four corners of the law is a promise a taxpayer is entitled to rely on. Where the authority acts within its powers, the legitimate expectation it creates is lawful, and resiling from it is what the court will not allow.

A reclassification runs forward, not back

The second holding is the one importers should pin to the wall. A reclassification runs forward, not back. The court held that the demand for the period after the vacating letter could not stand, because the Commissioner had expressly confined any change of tariff to future importations. A later opinion, even one from the World Customs Organization that vindicates the Commissioner's view of the correct heading, does not reach back and tax imports already cleared under the earlier ruling. The reasoning sits on top of a long line on how tax measures are construed. A taxing provision is read strictly, with nothing implied and nothing read in, and where its words are capable of two readings the one that imposes the lighter burden on the taxpayer prevails. The court applied that discipline not only to statutes but to the Commissioner's own letter, resolving the ambiguity the Commissioner argued for against him. It drew the principle from the classic authority, Cape Brandy Syndicate, and from the Supreme Court, which has restated for Kenya that taxing legislation must be strictly construed, without intendment or inference, and that doubt is read in the subject's favour.

Evidence is not there for the asking

The third holding is procedural, and it is a trap the revenue authority set for itself. The High Court had leaned on the minutes of a 2013 meeting that appeared to record an agreement that duty would run from 2008. Those minutes were never part of the Commissioner's pleadings before the Tribunal. They were introduced later, through a statement of facts, without any application for leave to adduce fresh evidence. The Court of Appeal held that additional evidence on appeal is not there for the asking. A party that wants to bring in material that was not before the first tribunal must apply for leave, and the court will admit it only on the established conditions, that it could not have been obtained earlier with reasonable diligence, that it would probably have an important influence on the result, and that it is credible. By acting on minutes that had slipped in without that gate being passed, the High Court, in the Court of Appeal's words, fell into error. A revenue authority cannot build its case on the appeal record it wishes it had filed below.

Why it matters now

The timing of this judgment gives it a longer reach than a single importer's refund. The Commissioner's power to seek a second opinion, and to revisit a classification in light of it, is now a formal part of the customs landscape. The authority has built out its review machinery, with an independent objections review introduced in 2025 and a technical review unit promised for customs disputes, and second opinions on tariff and value are becoming routine rather than exceptional. That makes the question this case answers a live one. When the authority changes its mind about a heading, from what date does the new view bite. The Court of Appeal's answer is that it bites from the change, not before, at least where the authority has said as much and the importer has relied on it.

For a business that imports under a settled tariff classification, the practical lessons are concrete and worth internalising. Get any concession from the authority in writing, in terms that are clear about what is being given up and from when, because a vacating letter in plain words is what carried this appeal. Keep those letters, because the fight years later turns on them. If the authority reclassifies a product and tries to reach back over imports already cleared, the starting position is that a reclassification is prospective, and the burden is on the authority to show a clear and lawful basis for anything else. And read every ambiguity in a demand, and in the authority's own correspondence, in the light the courts now read it, strictly, and where genuinely unclear, in the taxpayer's favour.
