A duty to police, with no safe harbour: the legal holes in the draft platform rules

The draft Kenya Information and Communications (Amendment) Bill 2026 would hand the Communications Authority a new hold over anyone who runs an online platform. Its new section 85C is built on ground that would not hold steady: broad, undefined duties, a criminal penalty behind them, and none of the safe harbour that makes platform rules workable.

The new section 85C of the draft Kenya Information and Communications (Amendment) Bill 2026 would make every operator of a digital platform police, report and answer for what its users do. For a lawyer the interest is not the targets but the design, and the design is missing the parts that would make platform regulation both workable and lawful.

No safe harbour, and a duty to monitor

Start with what would not be there. The settled approach to online intermediaries is that they are not liable for user content they neither know of nor control, and are not required to watch everything their users do. Liability attaches once they know of something unlawful and fail to act. Section 85C would go the other way. It would require a provider to ensure the safety of its service and to report any unlawful activity on it, with no knowledge threshold, no notice-and-takedown step and no safe harbour. Read as drafted, that is a general duty to monitor, the very thing mature regimes avoid, because it would turn private operators into surveillors of their own users and guarantors of conduct they cannot fully see.

Vague duties, with a criminal penalty behind them

Then there is the vagueness, and what would sit behind it. The section would define almost none of its key terms. The safety of a service, unlawful activity, even compliance with the relevant laws of Kenya, would all be left open. Vague standards can sometimes be cured by regulation, but the Bill would supply a criminal consequence. Because section 85C would carry no penalty of its own, a breach would fall to the Bill's proposed general penalty, a fine of up to one million shillings or imprisonment of up to three years. A duty whose content a person cannot know in advance, enforced by prison, would meet two old objections at once: a criminal offence must be defined clearly enough to guide conduct, and any limit on a protected right, here free expression, must be clear and proportionate. Neither sits easily with a command as open as "report any unlawful activity".

No knowledge, and no defence

The fault line would run deeper still. The section attaches no mental element. It would require a provider to report unlawful activity and the steps taken, but would not say the provider must have known of it, and would give no reasonable-steps defence. Pushed through the general penalty, that would edge towards criminal liability for an omission the provider may never have been aware of. A platform that missed a single unlawful post could, on the face of the section, be exposed. Sound drafting would add a knowledge threshold and a due-diligence defence. The Bill has neither.

Three duties pulling against each other

The duty would also land on ground that is already occupied. What counts as unlawful activity online is set by the existing criminal law, which has its own offences and its own routes to take content down. The user particulars and complaint records a provider would have to hold and hand over are personal data, governed by the Data Protection Act, which the Bill elsewhere expressly preserves. Section 85C would add a third line of duty, running to the Communications Authority, without reconciling it with either. A provider told to report a user to the Authority, to protect that same user's data, and to respect the user's expression, would be handed obligations that can pull against one another, with no rule on which yields.

None of this says platform regulation is wrong in principle. The online harms behind the Bill are real. It says the instrument, as drafted, would leave the courts to fill gaps the drafter should close first: a knowledge-based safe harbour, defined duties, a fault element, and a clear boundary between this regime, the cybercrime law and data protection. The Bill is still a draft, and nothing in it is settled. These are the points worth raising in a submission now, rather than litigating section 85C once it is law.
