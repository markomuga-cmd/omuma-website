Who controls Nairobi’s skyline: the Supreme Court draws the line

The Supreme Court has settled the Mbaazi Avenue high-rise fight, and the result reads like a contradiction. The policy Nairobi used to wave the towers through was never passed into law. The towers are going up anyway. The judgment resets what a county must do before it can zone, and what a neighbour must prove to stop a build.

It began as a quarrel between neighbours. Millennium Gardens Management, which runs a small residential block on Mbaazi Avenue in the Thompson part of Lavington, objected when a developer, Metricon, started putting up three sixteen-storey towers next door, five hundred and twelve flats on a single acre. The company and the local residents' association took it to court. They lost at the Environment and Land Court, lost again at the Court of Appeal, and on 30 October got the Supreme Court’s answer. The neighbours won a point of law. The towers are still going up. How both of those can be true is what makes the judgment worth reading.

The rulebook was never a law

Everything turned on one document, the Nairobi City County Development Control Policy of 2021. It is what the county and the two courts below had used to decide what could be built on the street, and it set the local skyline at sixteen floors. The Supreme Court looked at where that policy came from and found that the county’s own officials had drawn it up and begun using it, but the County Assembly had never passed it or gazetted it, which is what the Physical and Land Use Planning Act requires. Only the Assembly can turn a county policy into law. Until it does, the document is a working draft, and a working draft binds nobody. So the courts below had measured this development against a yardstick with no legal force.

A judge cannot decide a case on a document nobody argued

The way the policy came into the case made it worse. The trial judge said himself that he only came across the 2021 policy after the hearing had ended, and he then leaned on it heavily without asking either side to comment. The Supreme Court was firm that this will not do. A court may take judicial notice of a public document, but noticing that a document exists is not the same thing as deciding a case on it behind the parties' backs. If a point can swing the outcome, the parties have to be heard on it. The pleadings, not the judge’s later reading, set the boundaries of the fight.

So why are the towers still standing

Because the policy was only half the story. The Court made a point of saying that its finding on the 2021 policy did not decide the appeal, and the reason is plain. The developer had gone and obtained the approvals the law actually requires, a change of user, an environmental impact assessment licence, a National Construction Authority certificate, sign-off on water. Every one of them was still valid. None had been cancelled, and nobody had shown that any had been got by sharp practice. When a developer holds approvals like that and has acted in good faith, the law presumes they were properly given, and a court will not tear them up because the zoning policy behind them turned out to be soft. The foundation was shaky. The building permits were not.

Zoning is no longer just paperwork

There is a wider move in the judgment that planners and their lawyers should notice. The Court refused to treat zoning as a dry administrative matter. How tall a building may be, how many units it may hold, how densely a plot may be used, all of that decides how much light, air, water and road a neighbourhood has, and whether its drains and foundations can cope. That, the Court said, makes zoning one of the ways the State keeps its promise under Article 42 of a clean and healthy environment. A planning failure can now be dressed as a constitutional one, which is a larger thing to defend against.

What an objector actually has to show

The residents also learned where the limits sit. They had leaned on the precautionary principle, the idea that a regulator can act against a danger even before the science is settled. The Court agreed with the principle, then held that it does not switch on until someone shows a real risk of serious or lasting harm. Worries about lost sunlight, strained sewers and cracked walls, each answered on the file by an expert study and a licence condition, were not enough. And even where harm is real, the Court will rarely stop a project or bring one down. Money, or an order to scale the thing back or put the damage right, comes first. Demolition is kept for the clear cases.

Where the project goes from here

The Court did not kill the development. It handed it back to Nairobi City County to look at again, this time against the plan that now genuinely governs the area, the Nairobi City County Development Control Policy of 2026, which the Assembly passed and gazetted in June. The hole the case exposed, a city approving towers with no properly enacted plan behind them, has since been filled. So the project will be judged against the real rulebook, not the draft that caused all the trouble.

The practical reading

If you build, the thing that protects you is a clean set of statutory approvals, honestly obtained, not whatever informal policy the county happens to be working to that month. Check what instrument your permit actually rests on, but know that valid, unrevoked approvals are hard to dislodge. If you are buying into a tower or lending against one, the planning basis is now part of due diligence and not a box to tick, so ask what the approvals stand on and whether the Assembly ever passed it. If you run a county, the warning could not be plainer. An administration’s own policy is not law until the Assembly makes it one, and every approval built on an unpassed policy is open to attack. And if you are the neighbour who wants the tower stopped, the courts will make the county plan properly and will protect your right to be heard, but they will not pick the number of floors for it, they will not halt a build on fears you cannot prove, and they will not send in the bulldozers except in the clearest case.

Underneath all of it is a quiet point about devolution. Planning belongs to the county, done properly and with its Assembly behind it. The courts are there to hold it to that, not to draw the plan themselves.
