News & Events · Insurance & trade · 1 November 2026

No local marine cover, no clearance. The rule is not new; the enforcement is.

From 1 November, KRA will not clear an import unless it carries marine cargo insurance from a Kenyan-licensed insurer, logged in the customs system before you lodge the declaration. The duty to insure locally has been law since 2017. What changed is that it is now wired into the customs gate, and a foreign policy bought with your goods no longer counts.

From today, a container that arrives at Mombasa without local marine cargo insurance does not clear. The Kenya Revenue Authority has tied customs clearance to proof of cover from an insurer licensed here, issued as a Digital Marine Cargo Insurance Certificate and transmitted to its Integrated Customs Management System before a declaration can be lodged. No certificate, no clearance, and the cargo sits accruing storage and demurrage while the problem is sorted out.

The duty is not new

This is not a new tax or a new law. The obligation has sat in the statute book since 2017. Section 16A of the Marine Insurance Act, inserted by the Finance Act that year, requires a person with an insurable interest in marine cargo to place that insurance with an insurer registered under the Insurance Act, unless the Commissioner grants an exemption. For most of the years since, it was honoured in the breach. Importers kept insuring offshore, often as part of the price paid to the overseas seller, and nobody stopped the cargo. An earlier push to enforce the rule in early 2025 stalled on technical problems. What is different now is plumbing, not law: the certificate, the premium payment and the customs system have been joined up, so the requirement is checked by a machine at the gate rather than left to goodwill.

The reason the state cares is foreign exchange. Treasury estimates that Kenyan importers send somewhere around $200 million a year out of the country in marine premiums paid to foreign insurers, against a local market worth a fraction of that. Keeping that spend onshore is the policy, and the customs gate is how it is being enforced.

The trap is in your sale contract

Here is the part the coverage misses, and it is the part that will catch people. Whether this rule costs you money or merely costs you an afternoon depends on how you buy your goods.

If you import on CIF or CIP terms, your overseas seller arranges the insurance and builds it into the price. That cover is almost always placed with a foreign insurer, and from today it will not satisfy the local requirement. Your goods still will not clear, and you may find yourself buying a second, local policy on cargo you have already paid to insure. If you import on FOB or CFR terms, you were always going to arrange your own cover, so you simply place it locally and move on.

So the fix is a contract question before it is an insurance question. For regular shipments, review the Incoterm you buy on. Moving from CIF to FOB or CFR puts the insurance back in your hands, where you can place it locally and clear cleanly. If you have reasons to stay on CIF, the sale contract needs to say the cover must be placed with a Kenyan-licensed insurer, which an overseas seller may resist, or you accept that you will insure again at home and price for it. These are the kinds of terms worth fixing across a supply arrangement once, rather than fighting at the port each time a container lands.

What to do before the next container lands

The mechanics are straightforward once set up. Cover is bought against an active Import Declaration Form through a licensed insurer's portal, the KIFWA portal or eCitizen, the system pulls your cargo details from the customs database, the premium is paid through eCitizen, and the certificate is issued and mapped back to the customs system. Two things trip people up. A policy on its own is not enough: it has to be processed, paid and linked to the customs system before the declaration goes in. And the certificate details have to match the import documents exactly, or clearance stalls on the mismatch.

For anyone who imports regularly, the sensible steps are to set up the relationship with a local insurer now, brief your clearing agent so the certificate is in hand before the declaration, and build the extra step into your clearing timeline so it does not turn into demurrage. Where a genuine case exists, section 16A does allow the Commissioner to grant an exemption, which is worth exploring for unusual cargo or cover that cannot be placed locally.

This is general information, not legal advice. If you import on CIF terms or run standing supply contracts, speak to us about your Incoterms and insurance clauses before your next shipment, not after it is stuck at the port.
