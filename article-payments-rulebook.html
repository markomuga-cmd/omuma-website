Kenya is rewriting the payments rulebook. Here is what it means for you.

The draft law that would replace the 2011 regime behind mobile money and card payments hands the Central Bank sweeping new powers, prises open closed systems, and puts real capital behind every licence. Here is what the Bill actually says.

The National Treasury and the Central Bank of Kenya have published the draft National Payment System Bill, 2026, the biggest overhaul of payments regulation since the National Payment System Act of 2011 (Cap. 491A), which it repeals. It was published for public comment in September 2026 and the comment window closed on 9 October 2026, so the shape of the law is being settled now. The figures and sections below are drawn from the Bill itself.

Four things in it matter most to anyone who moves money in Kenya.

The Central Bank gets real supervisory teeth

Authorised officers will be able to enter and inspect a payment firm's premises with or without notice and retain its records (section 20). Where a firm fails its customers, breaches a directive or breaks the Act, the Central Bank can intervene under section 21: appoint a statutory manager to take over, direct the removal of an officer or employee, install a director who cannot be removed without its approval, restrict new business, and order agency arrangements to be ended. Administrative fines reach KES 20 million for a firm and KES 3 million for an officer, with a further KES 100,000 for each day a breach continues (section 71).

Open finance and interoperability stop being optional

Every provider and operator will have to run systems "capable of securely sharing customer data with third parties for open finance purposes," and the Central Bank may require a consent-based data-sharing mechanism (section 29). Systems must also be interoperable, arrangements need the Central Bank's prior approval, and it can compel interoperability by notice (section 28). In plain terms, the closed loops around mobile money and bank accounts are being opened up so licensed competitors can build on top of them.

Customer money must be ring-fenced

Electronic money issuers and wallet providers will have to hold all customer funds in a trust account at a licensed bank or microfinance bank (section 36). No more than KES 500 million, or 25% of the trust monies, whichever is higher, may sit in any one bank, and the money may be invested only in Government securities or interest-bearing trust accounts. Transfers into the firm's own operating account are prohibited, the funds cannot be commingled, and they are shielded from the firm's creditors and from its insolvency (sections 37 to 39).

Licensing is rebuilt, with real capital behind it

The Bill creates ten licence categories across payment service providers and payment system operators, each with its own minimum capital (Third Schedule, read with section 10). Core capital is defined as fully paid-up ordinary share capital plus disclosed reserves, less goodwill and intangibles (section 10(4)).

A firm that holds several categories must meet the highest category's minimum plus half of the minimum for each additional category (section 10(6)). The jump to KES 250 million for an electronic money issuer is the kind of figure that reshapes a business plan. Virtual asset firms are drawn in too: a virtual asset service provider licensed to provide payment services under the Virtual Asset Service Providers Act, 2025 falls within this Bill (section 4).

What to do now

If you run or advise a payment service provider or fintech, map your actual activities to these categories and capital thresholds, and build the trust-account and reporting duties into how you operate. If you are a bank, open finance means others will be entitled to build on your rails, so the consent and data-sharing work starts now, not on commencement. The Bill is still a draft and has not been tabled, but once enacted, existing operators get one year to comply (section 79). That clock is shorter than it sounds.
