News & Events · Financial services & payments · 31 October 2026

Kenya is rewriting the payments rulebook. Here is what it means for you.

The draft law that would replace the regime governing mobile money and card payments since 2011 hands the Central Bank sweeping new powers and prises open closed systems.

The National Treasury and the Central Bank of Kenya have published the draft National Payment System Bill, 2026, the most significant overhaul of payments regulation since the National Payment System Act of 2011. It was released for comment on 21 September and the consultation window closed on 9 October, so the shape of the law is being settled now.

Three changes matter most to anyone who moves money in Kenya. The first is supervisory muscle. The Bill would let the Central Bank inspect a payment firm's premises with or without notice, remove its officers, restrict its activities, and in serious cases appoint a statutory manager to take control.

The second is open finance. With a customer's consent, payment providers could be required to share data securely with licensed third parties, and interoperability would become mandatory. That cracks open the closed loops around mobile money and bank accounts so competitors can build on top of them.

The third is money safety. Electronic money issuers and wallet providers would have to hold all customer funds in ring-fenced trust accounts at licensed banks, separate from their own operating money, with limits on how much sits in any single bank.

The licensing architecture is being rebuilt around roughly ten categories, with minimum capital running from about KES 5 million for lighter services such as payment initiation and account information, up to KES 250 million for electronic money issuers. A firm holding several licences would need to meet the highest threshold plus half of each additional category. Virtual asset service providers that offer payment services would be pulled into the same framework.

So what should you do now. If you run or advise a payment service provider or fintech, map your actual activities to the new licence categories and capital thresholds, because the jump to KES 250 million for electronic money is the kind of number that reshapes a business plan. If you are a bank, open finance means others will be entitled to build on your rails, so the work on consent and data-sharing infrastructure starts now, not on commencement.

The Bill is still a draft and has not been tabled, but once enacted, existing operators would have twelve months to comply. That clock is shorter than it sounds.
