Riba and proof: why a bank recovered only what it lent

A bank sued on an Islamic financing facility and the borrower never turned up to defend it. The bank still walked away with its principal and nothing else, no profit and no interest. A recent High Court judgment shows why a Shariah-compliant facility is enforced on its own terms, and what a lender has to prove to recover more than the money it put in.

A bank advanced money under an Islamic financing facility, the borrower defaulted, and when the bank came to court the borrower did not even show up to argue. On paper that is the easiest kind of case to win. Yet the bank recovered only the sum it had lent, and lost its claim for profit and its claim for interest outright. A recent judgment of the High Court shows why a Shariah-compliant facility is enforced strictly on its own terms, and why a court will not quietly turn it into an ordinary interest-bearing loan to help a creditor out.

What the facility was

The money went out under a Musharakah, a profit-and-loss sharing arrangement built on a series of letters of offer, to fund a joint venture tied to a social housing project in Nakuru. The bank put in the capital and the borrower was to repay the investment together with an agreed share of the profit when the venture matured or defaulted. Two individuals backed the facility with joint and several guarantees. When the borrower stopped paying, the bank issued statutory demands, accelerated the debt and called on the guarantees. None of that was answered, and the borrower and the guarantors never filed a defence.

The principal was the easy part

Because the debt had two parts, the court treated them differently. A fixed, mathematically certain sum is a liquidated claim, and a court can enter final judgment for it on default. A claim for an unspecified share of profit is unliquidated, and liability aside, the amount has to be proved at a formal hearing before the court will put a figure on it. So the bank obtained an early default judgment for the liquidated principal, a little over 146 million shillings, while the profit claim was held back for proof. That split is routine, and it is where the case turned.

The profit had to be proved, and it was not

At the formal proof the bank’s witness produced the ledgers, the debentures and the guarantees, but led no evidence at all on the profit the venture had actually generated. The court had no basis on which to award it. Parties are bound by the four corners of the bargain they struck, and a court cannot rewrite a contract or invent a figure the evidence does not support. Under a Musharakah the return comes from real, variable profit and loss, not from a rate fixed in advance, so the profit is a fact to be demonstrated, not a number to be assumed. With nothing before it, the court dismissed the profit claim as unproved. The lender won the case and still left the upside on the table, purely because it did not prove it.

Why the court would not simply add interest

The bank had also asked for conventional commercial interest, and here the court drew the sharpest line. Islamic finance rests on the prohibition of riba, the charging of interest. To bolt conventional interest onto a Shariah-compliant Musharakah, the judge held, would rewrite the agreement and convert a permitted equity partnership into a prohibited interest-bearing debt. A court that enforces an Islamic facility enforces it as an Islamic facility; it does not reach outside the agreed framework to give a lender a remedy the contract was designed never to carry. An earlier decision made the same point, that a borrower who freely accepts Shariah terms will be held to them and a court will not manufacture sympathy for a defaulter, but it cuts both ways, and here it cut against the bank. Where an Islamic contract does allow for the cost of default, it does so through its own mechanism, a late-payment compensation known as Ta’widh, which this facility did not invoke.

Statute fills the gap, but only after judgment

That left the question of how the recovered sum earns anything at all while it remains unpaid. The answer separates the life of the contract from the life of the decree. During the contractual period the Shariah terms govern, and they carry no interest. Once the court has entered judgment, a different source takes over: interest at the statutory court rate attaches to the decree as a matter of law, not as a term of the contract, and runs until the debt is paid. The lender is not left without a time cost on its money, but that cost comes from the statute that governs every judgment debt, not from a rate smuggled back into a contract that forbids one.

What it means for lenders

The practical lessons are concrete, and they are about documentation and proof rather than doctrine. If a facility is Shariah-compliant, plead it as one and prove it as one. The profit element is the return on the whole arrangement, so build it into the facility documents in a way that can be evidenced, and bring that evidence to court, because an unproved profit is a lost profit even in an undefended case. Do not ask a court for conventional interest on an Islamic facility; it will not grant it, and the ask signals that the facility was not understood on its own terms. If the concern is the cost of default, provide for Ta’widh in the documents rather than relying on a court to improvise. And remember the dividing line the judgment draws: the contract governs until judgment and the statute governs after it, so the recoverable sum is shaped long before the matter reaches a courtroom, in the way the facility is written.
