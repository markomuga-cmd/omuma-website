Why a broken occupation sinks an adverse possession claim

Long occupation of another person's land can mature into ownership, but only if it is never interrupted. A recent Nairobi ruling is a reminder that the law protects continued possession, not the length of the story.

It is one of the counter-intuitive corners of Kenyan land law that a person who holds no title can, by occupying someone else's land long enough, come to own it, and that a registered proprietor can lose land by doing nothing with it for too long. That is adverse possession. The idea unsettles people who treat a title deed as the last word, but it rests on a clear statutory scheme and an equally clear set of conditions, and the most demanding of those conditions is that the occupation must never be broken. A ruling at the Environment and Land Court in Nairobi early this month, refusing to shield a group of Njiru residents who claimed a large parcel by long occupation, is a useful occasion to set out how the doctrine works, because the residents came undone on exactly that point. The main ownership suit between them and the company that holds the title is still pending, so what follows is about the law, not the merits of that case.

What the twelve years actually does

The statutory starting point is section 7 of the Limitation of Actions Act, which says an action to recover land may not be brought after twelve years. Once that period has run against an owner who has been out of possession, the owner's title is extinguished by the lapse of time, and the Act allows the person who has been in possession to apply to the High Court to be registered as proprietor in the owner's place. So the twelve years does real work. It does not merely bar a stale claim, it moves the land from one person to another.

That outcome is not a historical oddity the courts tolerate grudgingly. In Mtana Lewa v Kahindi Ngala Mwagandi the Court of Appeal turned away the argument that adverse possession offends the constitutional right to property, holding that the right protected by Article 40 is not one of the absolute rights and may be limited by law, and that adverse possession is a legitimate, non-arbitrary mechanism that promotes certainty of title and discourages stale claims. Land lost in this way is lost lawfully. What the courts have insisted on instead is that the possession relied on be the real thing.

The condition that decides most claims

Those standards are exacting, and the Court of Appeal has set them out more than once. In Gachuma Gacheru v Maina Kabuchwa the court held that a claimant must prove, on a balance of probabilities, possession that is open, exclusive and adverse to the owner, enjoyed as of right, which the older cases put in the Latin as nec vi, nec clam, nec precario, taken without force, without secrecy and without the owner's permission. Permission is fatal, because a person who occupies by leave of the owner is not dispossessing anyone and time never begins to run. And the possession, the court said, must be continuous, and must not be broken for any temporary purpose or by any endeavour to interrupt it. That last requirement is where most claims, and the Njiru claim, fall apart.

Continuity matters because the whole doctrine is built on an owner sleeping on their rights for an unbroken stretch of twelve years, and the Limitation of Actions Act spells out what happens when that stretch is broken. Section 13 provides that where land ceases to be in adverse possession before the twelve years is complete, the time already run is undone, and a fresh period begins only if and when someone again takes adverse possession. So if the owner re-enters, or evicts the occupier, or the occupier gives up possession before the period matures, the clock does not pause, it is defeated, and any later occupation starts again from nothing. An eviction carried out under a court order is the clearest interruption there is. It is the owner asserting the very right that adverse possession would have punished them for neglecting, and it resets the position entirely. That is why, in the Njiru matter, the occupiers' real difficulty was not the length of their story but the gap in it. Having been removed from the land under a court order some years earlier, they could not show the continuous, present possession the doctrine demands.

Why an interim order was no help

The same point decided the application they actually brought, which was not the ownership claim itself but a request for interim orders to hold off eviction while that claim is heard. A court asked to preserve a position by injunction needs something still in existence to preserve, and a prima facie case that the applicant holds it. An occupier who is already off the land is not asking the court to keep things as they are, but to restore a state of affairs that has ended, which an interim order is not designed to do. Showing identifiable, current occupation was therefore the threshold, and it was the one they could not cross.

The lesson runs both ways

For the two kinds of client the firm acts for, the lesson runs in opposite directions from the same rule. A registered owner should treat a title as something to be defended rather than filed away, because open and uninterrupted occupation by another, left unchallenged for twelve years, can quietly extinguish ownership. The answer is to watch the land and to act within time, and a documented re-entry, eviction or suit stops the period before it matures. An occupier who believes long use has earned a right should understand that the use must have been continuous, exclusive, open and unpermitted, and that a single effective interruption, above all an eviction, can undo years of it.
