Why Quickmart's own founders were turned away from its IPO

Quickmart's share sale drew far more money than it could take, and when the shares were handed out the retail public got every share it asked for while the company's own founders got none. That ranking was not improvised on results day. Kenya's public-offer rules make a company write a fair and equitable allocation policy before the offer opens and have the regulator approve it, and Quickmart's results show exactly how such a policy decides who is made whole and who absorbs the squeeze.

Quickmart's initial public offer, an offer for sale of two billion existing shares at KES 7.50 each by its controlling shareholder, Sokoni Retail Kenya Limited, closed heavily oversubscribed. The company's own results announcement records 18,492 applications for about 2.45 billion shares worth KES 18.4 billion, against the two billion on offer, an overall subscription level of 122.7 per cent. When the shares were shared out, the pattern was stark. Retail investors were allocated every share they applied for. Local and regional institutions, the same. The founder shareholders, who had put in five applications for 118.6 million shares worth close to KES 890 million, were allocated nothing at all. The announcement puts it plainly, that because the offer was oversubscribed, no offer shares were allocated to the founder shareholders who applied.

A rule written before the offer opened

This was not a snub dreamed up when the applications were counted. The decision rule had been fixed months earlier and signed off by the regulator. The framework in force is the Capital Markets (Public Offers, Listings and Disclosures) Regulations, 2023, which replaced the 2002 rules, and it lays down no single pro-rata formula. Instead it makes the company set its own method and publish it first. Regulation 22 obliges the issuer to establish and disclose, in the information memorandum, a fair and equitable allocation policy, and that memorandum is approved by the Capital Markets Authority under Regulation 18. Quickmart's results announcement says as much in terms, recording that the oversubscribed shares were allocated in accordance with the fair and equitable allocation policy disclosed in the offer documents pursuant to regulation 22(1) of those regulations. The sorting that produced winners and losers was the application of a published rule, not a late improvisation.

What the policy put first

What that rule put first is now visible in the numbers, and it repays a close look. Retail and local institutional investors were filled in full, at a hundred per cent. The cornerstone investors, who had come in early with guaranteed allocations, were trimmed slightly, to about ninety per cent between them. Foreign investors, whose own pool was nearly three times oversubscribed, absorbed most of the shortfall and came away with under half of what they sought. One cornerstone backer even gave back about five million dollars of its guaranteed shares so that local institutions and foreign applicants could receive more. And the founders, at the very back of the queue, got nothing. Read down that list and the policy's preferences are unmistakable, to protect the ordinary public and the local market first, to lean on the investors best able to bear a cut, and to keep insiders out of the public tranche altogether.

Why the founders come last

That last choice is not harsh, it is structural. A listing exists to move shares into public hands, and the regulations put that in hard numbers. For the Main Investment Market Segment, the First Schedule requires a free float of at least fifteen per cent of the securities, held by a minimum of 250 shareholders, and it excludes controlling shareholders and senior managers from the count. The whole purpose of the offer is to build that public base. Founders buying back through the public queue would shrink the very float the sale was meant to create. The regulations treat insiders as a different animal throughout, so that an offer confined to controlling or substantial shareholders or directors is not a public offer at all but an exempt private one under Regulation 17. When those insiders line up alongside the public, a fair and equitable policy can properly, and here did, put them last.

The lessons, for issuers, founders and investors

For anyone heading to the market, or buying into a company that does, the lessons are concrete. A founder or existing shareholder who wants to hold or build a stake should do it on the pre-offer register, through the shares retained and the agreed lock-ups, not through the public application, where the allocation policy can zero them out. An investor should read the allocation policy in the memorandum before applying, because that clause, and not the headline level of demand, decides the fill. The foreign applicants who received under half their shares and the retail investors who received all of theirs were treated differently by design, and the design was published in advance. And for the advisers who build an offer, the allocation policy is a governance instrument, not a formality. It is where the protection of small investors, the guard against concentration, and the treatment of insiders are either set out clearly or left to cause trouble later. Quickmart's founders met the gentle version of that rule, an allocation of nothing. The severe version is a contested allotment, and the policy is written precisely to keep an offer clear of it.
