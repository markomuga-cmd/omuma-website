Airtel listed its mobile money arm to settle a promise, not to raise money

The London float raised nothing for Airtel. It was there to give its 2021 minority investors an exit and lift a put option worth up to $515 million off the balance sheet. For anyone taking or giving minority investment, the lesson is in how that exit was written.

On 14 October Airtel Africa listed its mobile money business on the London Stock Exchange, priced at £1.96 a share and valued at about £5.3 billion, or $7 billion. It was the largest London listing in more than a year. Read as a flotation it looks like a triumph. Read as a legal event it is more instructive, because Airtel sold none of its own shares and took none of the money. The company raised about $703 million, and every dollar went to the minority investors who were selling, not to the business. The shares then slipped 6.1 per cent on the first day. The point of the exercise was never new capital. It was to honour a promise made in 2021, and to remove the liability that promise had created.

The clause that set the clock

In 2021 Airtel sold about 22 per cent of its mobile money holding company, Airtel Mobile Commerce BV, to four investors, Qatar Holding, Mastercard, The Rise Fund and Chimetech Holding, for a combined $550 million. That valued the unit at about $2.65 billion. The money helped Airtel cut debt and put a separate price on a business it wanted to show off.

The investors did not buy on trust. Two of them, The Rise Fund and Mastercard, took a put option: if Airtel did not list the business within four years, they could require it to buy their shares back, at a fair value set by an agreed merchant bank and subject to a cap. A put option is simply the right to sell at a set trigger. In a shareholder agreement it is the minority investor’s insurance, a way out that does not depend on finding a willing buyer. For the company on the other side it is a contingent liability, a cash call that may never come but has to be carried as if it might. Airtel carried it at $515 million as at March 2026, down from $542 million a year earlier, the fall coming not from any change in the right but from dividends the agreement nets off against it.

Deadlines have a price

The four-year window closed in July 2025 without a listing. Airtel negotiated a twelve-month extension to 31 July 2026, then postponed again in May 2026, citing market conditions. When the extended deadline passed, the put holders were free to demand their cash. That is the pressure behind an October listing that might otherwise have waited for a kinder market.

And the market was not kind. The sellers had hoped for a valuation of $8 to $9 billion. They priced at about $7 billion, and the stock still fell on the first day. A hard exit deadline, once it is public, is known to everyone sitting across the table, and the side that has to transact by a fixed date is the side with the weaker hand. Airtel chose a listing at a disappointing price over a buy-back of up to $515 million in cash, which tells you how the two options compared. The exit right did its job for the investors, whose stakes were worth around $1.5 billion by listing day against the $550 million they put in. It did it at the company’s expense.

What this means for deals done here

None of this is peculiar to a London mega-float. Put and call options, listing covenants, fair-value formulas and lock-in periods are the ordinary furniture of private equity rounds, venture investments, joint ventures and family holding structures, including the ones we paper in Nairobi. They are often drafted far more loosely than Airtel’s were, and the loose drafting is found only when someone tries to use them.

A few points carry across. For a company raising money, the exit terms you concede are not a formality to settle last. A put option with a cash trigger is a liability that can drive your largest decisions years later, as it drove this one, so the deadline, the valuation mechanism, the cap and the events that start the clock deserve as much attention as the headline price. For an investor taking a minority stake the reverse holds. A clean, well-drafted exit is what turns a minority position into one you can actually leave, on terms you can predict, rather than a holding you are locked into at the majority’s pleasure.

There is a Kenyan footnote. Part of what unsettled London investors was regulation they could not model, and the example they reached for was Kenya’s own draft National Payment System Bill, which would require customer wallet balances to be held in trust and invested only in government securities or interest-bearing deposits. A rule written for consumer protection in Nairobi turns up as a pricing risk in a London prospectus. For a pan-African payments business the regulatory text in each market is part of the valuation, not a detail beneath it.

Airtel also chose London over any African exchange, for the plain reason that no market on the continent could absorb a $7 billion float. That is its own comment on where African capital markets stand, and a question worth sitting with as more of the continent’s fintech reaches this size.
