Kenya's biggest banks face a new capital surcharge, and the comment window shuts Friday

The Central Bank has put a sweeping revision of its prudential and risk management rules out for public comment, together with a framework that would single out the banks whose failure could threaten the whole system and make them hold more capital. Comments close on Friday, 7 November.

On 10 September the Central Bank of Kenya invited public comment on a package that reshapes how banks are regulated: revised Prudential Guidelines, revised Risk Management Guidelines, a set of Guidance Notes, and a new framework for identifying, regulating and supervising domestic systemically important banks. The window to respond closes this Friday, 7 November.

The banks that are too important to fail

The centrepiece is the D-SIBs framework, Kenya's take on the global rules for banks whose failure would not stay contained. The thinking is straightforward. Some banks are large and connected enough that their collapse would ripple through the whole system, so they should carry a thicker cushion. The framework sets out how the Central Bank will find them. It scores every licensed bank on five measures, its size, how interconnected it is with the rest of the financial system, how hard the services it provides would be to replace, how complex it is, and how important it is to the domestic economy, with size weighing the heaviest. A bank that crosses the threshold is designated and placed in one of three buckets. The assessment runs on an annual cycle, with the list of named banks published each year.

The capital surcharge, and what comes with it

A designated bank must hold extra core capital, over and above the normal minimums and the capital conservation buffer, a surcharge of 0.5, 1.5 or 2.5 percent of its risk-weighted assets depending on its bucket. The capital has to be common equity, the highest quality there is. A named bank gets twelve months to comply and must file a board-approved plan within three months of being told.

The surcharge is only part of it. A systemically important bank would also have to prepare recovery and resolution plans, the playbook for how it would be stabilised or wound down in a crisis, and refresh them every year. It would face more intense supervision, including quarterly stress tests. And the Central Bank is asking for the power to tell such a bank to hold back on expanding or launching new products where that would add to systemic risk, a notable reach into commercial strategy. Around all of this sit the revised Prudential and Risk Management Guidelines and the Guidance Notes, which update the everyday rules on governance, risk and capital for every bank, not only the large ones.

Who should be reading this, and what to do before Friday

The obvious audience is the handful of large banks likely to be named, for whom a surcharge of up to 2.5 percent of risk-weighted assets is real money and a direct claim on capital that might otherwise support lending or dividends. But the guidelines reach the whole sector, so every bank's board and risk function has something to check. The comment stage is the point at which the rules can still be shaped, from where the thresholds are set to how quickly the extra capital must be found.

The Central Bank wants comments on its template, emailed to fin@centralbank.go.ke, or delivered in hard copy to the Director, Bank Supervision Department. The drafts are on the Central Bank's website. If your institution has a view on where the line is drawn or how fast the capital must be built, this is the week to put it on record. And if you will not be commenting, it is still worth knowing what is coming, because these rules are heading for adoption either way.
