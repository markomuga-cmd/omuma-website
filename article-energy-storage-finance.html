A grant and a loan to 2048: the finance behind Kenya’s first grid batteries

KenGen has secured about 10.5 billion shillings to build Kenya’s first grid-scale battery storage, almost all of it a climate-fund grant and a concessional World Bank loan that runs to 2048. The structure is the real story, and it explains both why the project is happening and the two questions worth keeping in view.

Kenya Electricity Generating Company has lined up about 10.5 billion shillings to build the country’s first grid-scale battery storage, and the way the money is put together is more interesting than the headline number. Almost all of it is concessional. The Green Climate Fund is providing a grant of roughly 45 million dollars, and the World Bank’s International Development Association is lending the balance, around 32 million euros, on soft terms, with the loan not maturing until 2048. KenGen has been appointed by the World Bank as the implementing agent, and the batteries, about 100 megawatts and 100 megawatt-hours, will sit at Olkaria, Kipevu, Embakasi and Muhoroni.

Kenya generates a lot of wind and solar, but it cannot store it. When the wind drops in the evening as demand peaks, the grid falls back on expensive thermal plants and on imported power. Batteries let a utility hold the cheap midday solar and the strong afternoon wind and release it when it is needed, which is why KenGen calls the lack of storage a brake on its whole renewable strategy.

The finance is the story

Storage is hard to fund commercially because, in Kenya’s market as it stands, a battery does not yet have a clear revenue stream of its own the way a power plant selling electricity does. That is exactly the gap concessional climate finance is built to fill. A grant that never has to be repaid, stacked on a loan priced below the market and stretched over more than two decades, lowers the cost of capital far enough to make a first-of-its-kind asset bankable. This is the standard template for early infrastructure, and it is worth understanding because it is how a good deal of Kenya’s energy transition will be paid for. For developers, independent power producers and their financiers, the signal is where the cheap money sits, in the climate funds and the development finance institutions, and that for now the state utility is the vehicle carrying the pilot.

Two questions worth keeping in view

A sober look leaves two fair questions on the table, neither of which takes anything away from the step forward. The first is scale. One hundred megawatt-hours is about an hour of cover at full output, which is modest against the wind and solar it is meant to firm, and KenGen itself is already talking about reaching 500 megawatt-hours in the next few years. So this is a pilot, and the real test is whether the follow-on capacity arrives and how it is financed once the grant money is gone. The second is value. The installed cost of battery storage has been falling quickly and is expected to keep falling, so the price of anything bought today deserves scrutiny and will look dearer in a few years than it does now. The honest qualification is that the headline figure here covers several sites, the grid works to connect them and an associated solar redevelopment, so it is not a clean price for the batteries alone, and a like-for-like comparison needs that detail.

Neither question is a reason to hold back. The significant thing in this deal is the financing template, a climate grant and a long concessional loan used to de-risk the asset that makes renewables dispatchable. If it works here, it is the shape of how Kenya funds the storage its grid will need at scale, and the terms on which that money comes will matter as much as the megawatts.
