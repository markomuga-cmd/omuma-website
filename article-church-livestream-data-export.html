The church filming debate has missed the bigger problem: a livestream is a data export

The consent argument is only half the law. When a service goes out on YouTube or Facebook, a Kenyan church is sending its congregation’s religious data to servers abroad, and that triggers a stricter test the pew notices cannot meet.

The outgoing Data Commissioner Immaculate Kassait has told churches that a notice at the door saying cameras are rolling does not, by itself, amount to consent, a position the Office had already committed to writing in its guidance on recording and publication. Her remark set off a public debate over consent, and several large congregations responded by posting filming notices and, in some cases, setting aside camera-free seats. The whole conversation is being held as a filming-consent question. It is two questions, and the second one is the harder of the two.

The footage is sensitive data, not ordinary data

The coverage treats a worshipper’s image as ordinary personal data. It is not. The section 2 definition of sensitive personal data in the Data Protection Act, 2019 covers data revealing a person’s belief. An identifiable shot of someone worshipping inside a named church reveals that person’s religious belief by its context alone. That single fact moves the entire exercise out of the ordinary lawful-basis menu in section 30, where legitimate interest lives, and into the narrow grounds for sensitive data in section 45, where it does not. A church cannot process identifiable congregants on a legitimate-interest theory, because for this category of data that theory is not available.

The livestream leaves Kenya

A service streamed to YouTube, Facebook or TikTok is hosted on servers outside Kenya. The ODPC’s own Guidance Note on Cross-border Data Transfers states that processing on servers located outside Kenya is a cross-border transfer. Publishing a service to a foreign platform is therefore a transfer of the congregation’s data abroad.

For ordinary data, section 48 asks for adequacy or appropriate safeguards. For sensitive data, section 49 is stricter. It requires the explicit consent of the data subject and confirmation of appropriate safeguards. Both, not either. A church livestream carries the most protected category of data across the border on the loosest possible arrangement.

Why the door-sign fails twice

The fashionable fix, a filming notice backed by a consent form at the entrance, does not complete the legal test, and it fails on two separate limbs.

On consent, the regulator has already closed the door. The ODPC’s Guidance Note for Processing on Publications of Recorded Media says that a privacy notice does not serve as consent for publication, which must be obtained separately, and that the handler should obtain consent from every individual who is identifiable in a livestream, on a form that lists transfer risk and the right to withdraw. A sign on the wall is not that. This is not theoretical. In a determination of 8 April 2026 the Office rejected the very same argument, where an events company that had used a woman’s image argued her consent came from the terms and conditions shared with its attendees. The Commissioner found there was no valid express consent and ordered the company to compensate her under section 65. A filming notice on a church wall is the same argument in a different building.

On safeguards, even a church that somehow collected perfect consent from every face in the room has satisfied only one of the two requirements in section 49. The safeguards limb remains, and no congregation has a data-transfer arrangement with a global platform that it has put before the Data Commissioner. Consent forms, however well drafted, do not reach this problem at all. The Office has also shown that it will compensate where sensitive data is passed to an outside party without proven consent, in a March 2026 determination against a hospital that sent a patient’s results to a referral laboratory it could not prove she had agreed to. A livestream sends the most sensitive data of all to a party much further away.

The consent itself is fragile

There is a further weakness under the consent limb. Section 2 requires consent to be free, and section 32 puts the burden of proving it on the church. A show of hands in the pew, taken under the minister’s eye and among one’s own community, is close to the textbook setting in which free is doubtful. A church that rests its compliance on collective consent gathered in that moment is building on sand, quite apart from the export problem sitting behind it.

The way out is in the regulator’s own example

The answer is not more paper. It is where the camera points. The Recorded Media guidance uses a church to make the point: it describes a congregation whose livestream focuses only on the pulpit and the sermon and excludes the people in the pews. If no congregant is identifiable, there is no processing of their belief and no export of it, and the consent and transfer questions fall away together. The compliant church is not the one with the fullest consent file. It is the one that has framed the shot so that its members are not in it.

Two qualifications are worth stating plainly for any church leadership. Children’s services sit under section 33, which requires parental consent, and the ODPC’s Guidance Note for Processing Children’s Data treats a child’s image as a heightened duty that the Office has been willing to enforce. The ODPC has not yet spelled out, in writing, that publishing to social media is a cross-border transfer. Its definition of a transfer and its treatment of foreign hosting lead there, but the point is one a church would be advancing, not one the regulator has already ruled. That is precisely why it is worth saying now.

For churches, the move is to zone the room rather than paper it, to keep the camera on the front, to take proper written consent only from those who choose to appear, the worship team and anyone giving a testimony, and to keep a working opt-out and takedown route for footage already online. The debate in the papers is about notices. The debate churches should be having is about where their members’ faith is being sent, and who is holding it once it gets there.
