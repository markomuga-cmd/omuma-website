Why a Kenyan clearance does not finish a cross-border bank deal

The Central Bank of Kenya and the Treasury cleared Access Bank’s purchase of National Bank of Kenya, and it completed, yet the bank still sits off its buyer’s books because the approval in Lagos is not finished. What that limbo means for a seller, a target and the regulator here.

National Bank of Kenya has been bought, and for most purposes it belongs to Access Bank of Nigeria. The Central Bank of Kenya approved the acquisition, the Cabinet Secretary for the National Treasury approved it, and the deal completed in May 2025. Yet when Access published its group accounts for the nine months to September 2026 it left NBK out, treating a bank it owns as an investment rather than a subsidiary and taking about Ksh157 billion of assets off its own balance sheet. The reason is not in Nairobi but in Lagos, where Access’s home regulator has not given the deal its unconditional blessing. The episode is a useful lesson in something Kenyan sellers and targets do not always price in. A clearance here does not finish a cross-border bank deal.

What Kenyan law settled

On the Kenyan side the acquisition is done and lawful. Taking control of a bank here needs the regulator’s approval, and the Central Bank of Kenya gave it, approving the purchase of all of NBK on 4 April 2025 under section 13(4) of the Banking Act. The Cabinet Secretary for the National Treasury approved it on 10 April 2025, with the related transfers of assets and liabilities handled under section 9, which requires the Cabinet Secretary’s prior written approval and that the transaction not be detrimental to the public interest. Once those approvals were in place and the transaction completed, Access became the owner of NBK, which remains a licensed Kenyan bank under the supervision of the Central Bank. Nothing about the Nigerian question changes that. NBK’s licence, its depositors and its day to day business sit squarely under Kenyan law.

What the home regulator left unsettled

What Kenyan approval could not deliver is the buyer’s own freedom to own what it has bought. The Central Bank of Nigeria has been tightening the leash on its banks' foreign ambitions, raising the minimum capital for international banks and, more to the point, capping what a Nigerian lender may hold in overseas subsidiaries at a share of its own funds, with a window to comply. Until Access satisfies those conditions, its regulator has withheld the unconditional approval the deal needs at home, and Access has responded by keeping NBK off its consolidated accounts.

That accounting choice is where the law and the numbers meet. Under the standard that governs group accounts, IFRS 10, a parent consolidates every entity it controls, control meaning the power to direct the business and exposure to its returns. A bank bought outright is the textbook case of a controlled subsidiary. Excluding it reads less like a genuine absence of control than a way of keeping a large foreign asset off the group balance sheet while the home capital rules bite. Whatever the merits of that treatment in Lagos, for the Kenyan institution it is a reminder that a buyer’s regulatory position at home can shape how, and whether, the Kenyan business is recognised as part of the group at all.

The structuring in between

The parties did not leave the gap unmanaged. To protect the seller while the Nigerian conditions are worked out, the deal was backed by a bill facility of up to about $106.7 million securing KCB’s right to be paid pending satisfaction of the regulatory conditions. That is the tell. A transaction can be complete in law, with every local approval in hand, and still not be economically finished, which is why a careful seller builds a bridge over the conditionality rather than trusting the closing to carry all the risk.

The lessons, for sellers, targets and the regulator

For a Kenyan seller or target in a cross-border bank deal, the practical point is that the foreign buyer’s home regulator is a party to the transaction in all but name, and its conditions can delay or reshape the deal long after the local clearance. That belongs in the bargain from the start, as conditions precedent that capture the home approvals, as payment security or an escrow for the seller, and as a clear allocation of who carries the cost and the risk of a home-country hold up, the ground the bill facility here was covering. For the Central Bank of Kenya the case raises a sharper supervisory question, how to approve a buyer whose own regulator has not finished, and what conditions protect the Kenyan bank, its depositors and the integrity of its balance sheet while the buyer’s home position is unresolved.

There is a larger current beneath all of this. Kenyan banks have become acquisition targets for larger regional and West African lenders, and those buyers carry their own home-country capital rules with them. A Kenyan clearance clears the Kenyan hurdle, not the whole race. In cross-border bank deals the home regulator can hold the finish line, and the parties who plan for that are the ones not left carrying the risk when it does.
