News & Events · Financial services & payments · 1 November 2026

Payment fees now carry 16% VAT. The taxman lost in court, so the law changed.

The Finance Act 2026 has stripped the VAT exemption from the fees that payment providers, gateways and banks charge to process your money. It is no accident of drafting. It undoes, by statute, a run of cases the taxman had just lost.

From this month, the fee you pay to move money electronically carries 16 percent VAT. The money itself is untouched. The charge for moving it is now taxable. Banks and fintechs began adding the tax to customer fees around 1 November, though the law behind it has been in force since 1 July. The change comes from the Finance Act 2026, and it closes a fight the Kenya Revenue Authority had been losing in court.

The taxman lost in court, so the law changed

For years the Authority argued that the fees payment firms earn are taxable technology services, not exempt financial services. It kept losing. In Pesapal Limited v Commissioner of Domestic Taxes the High Court held in August 2025 that a licensed provider's commissions are exempt financial services under the First Schedule to the VAT Act, and set aside the Authority's assessment of Sh76.8 million in tax with a further Sh33.9 million in penalties and interest. Weeks later the Tax Appeals Tribunal said the same for payment switching in the Kenswitch case.

Rather than appeal Pesapal, the Treasury rewrote the statute, removing from the exemption the very words those wins relied on. The same Finance Act does the same thing to a Supreme Court ruling on withholding tax on card and interchange payments. The pattern is plain. Where the revenue authority loses in court, the law is changed under it.

What is now taxed, and what is not

The exemption now splits the transaction in two. Moving the money stays exempt. The fee or commission for processing it is standard-rated at 16 percent. The tax reaches payment processing, settlement, merchant acquiring, gateway and aggregation services supplied over a platform by a payment service provider for a fee. Ordinary person-to-person transfers on M-Pesa and Airtel Money are left alone, a proposal to tax those was dropped before the Bill became law.

There is a sting most have missed. A mobile-money fee already carries 15 percent excise duty, and the new VAT applies on the excise-inclusive amount. The real bite on a fee is therefore more than sixteen points, not exactly sixteen.

July or November, and why it matters

The law has applied since 1 July. The banks only switched the charge on around 1 November. The text telling you the tax is "effective 1 November" marks the day your bank started collecting it, not the day the liability began. That leaves four months in which VAT was due but, for many providers, never charged. A provider that did not pass it on may find the Authority looking to it for the gap. The July to October exposure is worth checking now rather than later.

What to do about it

If you run a payment business, a gateway, a switch or a bank, map which of your fee lines are now standard-rated, because not every charge is caught. Then look hard at the VAT clause in your customer contracts: a contract that is silent or expressed VAT-exclusive pushes the tax onto the customer, one that is VAT-inclusive eats your margin. Fix your eTIMS invoicing so the tax is charged and claimed cleanly. There is one gain to bank, now that these supplies are taxable, the input VAT on the costs behind them becomes recoverable, which offsets part of the cost.

For merchants and consumers, the charge will mostly be passed down the line, so expect higher costs on card machines, tills and paybills, and price for it.

And it is not beyond challenge. The consumer body Cofek went to court over the Bill, but the case was struck out as premature because the Bill was not yet law. That door is still open: a fresh challenge to the Act on its merits remains possible, and no court has yet ruled on whether the tax is constitutional. For periods before 1 July, the reasoning in Pesapal and Kenswitch still supports an exemption, so older assessments are worth contesting on that footing.
