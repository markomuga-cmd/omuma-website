When the judgment that justified the tax is reversed

Absa Bank Kenya paid the tax it was ordered to pay, won on appeal five years later, and was told the money was not coming back. The Tax Appeals Tribunal disagreed, and the reasoning matters to any taxpayer who has ever paid under protest.

A taxpayer who loses in the Court of Appeal and pays up has not necessarily lost the money. That is the effect of a judgment the Tax Appeals Tribunal delivered on 6 November 2026, ordering the Kenya Revenue Authority to refund Sh317.2 million to Absa Bank Kenya, which had paid it six years earlier under a judgment since reversed.

The sum is not what makes the decision worth reading. What makes it worth reading is that the Tribunal had to answer a question that had not been squarely decided here before. What happens to tax that was lawfully collected under a court judgment, when the judgment that made it lawful is later set aside?

How the money came to be paid

The dispute began with an audit of Absa’s affairs covering 2007 to 2010, when it still traded as Barclays Bank of Kenya. The Commissioner treated the fees Absa paid to Visa, Mastercard and American Express as royalties, and the interchange it shared with card issuing banks as management or professional fees, and assessed withholding tax on both. The High Court quashed those decisions on judicial review in May 2015. The Court of Appeal reversed that in November 2020 and held both categories taxable.

No stay was granted. Twelve days after the Court of Appeal judgment the Commissioner demanded Sh626 million, made up of Sh317.2 million in principal, Sh31.7 million in penalties and Sh277 million in interest, and warned of enforcement if it was not settled. Absa paid the principal on 2 December 2020, told the Commissioner that payment was not an admission of liability, and carried on to the Supreme Court.

On 5 December 2025 the Supreme Court allowed the appeal and set the Court of Appeal judgment aside, holding that fees paid by an acquiring bank to the card companies are not royalties and that interchange paid to an issuing bank is not a management or professional fee. Absa asked for its money back. The Commissioner refused, on the ground that the tax had been collected while the Court of Appeal judgment was enforceable, and that the Supreme Court decision operated only from the date it was delivered.

Collecting and keeping are two different questions

The Tribunal’s answer to that is the heart of the judgment. The Commissioner’s position, it said, conflated whether he was entitled to collect the tax in 2020 with whether he is entitled to retain it after the reversal.

Nobody disputed the first. The Court of Appeal judgment was operative and the demand was enforceable. The second question is answered by the constitutional rule that no tax may be imposed except as provided by legislation, which requires a legislative basis for holding a sum as tax. Once the Court of Appeal judgment was set aside, it could no longer supply one. It is the same provision that sank a taxpayer who had relied on a government promise of exemption, and it runs in both directions. If the revenue cannot give tax away without legislation, it cannot keep it without legislation either.

That is why the argument about prospective effect failed. A court construing a statute does not legislate, it declares what the statute has meant since it was enacted, and the provisions in question did not change on 5 December 2025. Prospective overruling exists, but it is exceptional and a court has to invoke it expressly. The Supreme Court did not, and it made its orders knowing the tax had already been paid, because the Commissioner had told it so in his own submissions.

The Commissioner’s conduct did not help him. Within days of refusing this refund he signed consents revising similar assessments to nil for periods running from January 2017 to December 2022, all of them before the Supreme Court judgment. The Tribunal noted that he had given the judgment retrospective effect in one forum while denying it in another.

Overpaid does not mean paid by mistake

The Commissioner also argued that a payment made in compliance with a valid judgment is not an overpayment at all, because nothing had gone wrong when it was made.

The Tribunal read the refund provision of the Tax Procedures Act as it is written. It speaks of tax that has been overpaid, not of tax paid by mistake, and nothing in it confines refunds to payments known to be excessive at the time. A payment that was due under an operative judgment becomes an overpayment when the judgment that sustained it is reversed.

The structure of the Act supports that reading. Parliament dealt with tax paid in error separately, in a section of its own. Had the refund provision been meant to cover only mistakes, that later section would have had nothing left to do.

The clock runs from the reversal, not the payment

The limitation argument is the part of this judgment that will be cited most, and it turned on a single word.

The Act gives a taxpayer five years to claim a refund of overpaid income tax. The Commissioner argued the claim was out of time, and quoted a version of the section that runs the five years from the date on which the tax was paid. The text actually in force runs it from the date on which the tax was overpaid.

On 2 December 2020 the money was not an overpayment. The Commissioner himself insisted on that. It became one on 5 December 2025, when the judgment that sustained the liability was set aside, and the five years began then. The application, filed 83 days later, was comfortably in time.

The Tribunal pointed out what the Commissioner’s reading would have meant. The right would have expired on 2 December 2025, three days before the judgment that created it. Any earlier application would have been refused while the Court of Appeal judgment stood, as the Commissioner’s own submissions accepted. The law does not demand a futile application.

Where these appeals now go

One procedural point is worth more to most taxpayers than the rest of the judgment. Since the Finance Act 2023 removed refund decisions from the definition of a tax decision, a refusal to refund is an appealable decision that goes straight to the Tribunal. There is no objection stage to exhaust first, and the Act allows thirty days from notification of the decision.

The Commissioner argued that the Tribunal could not rule on what a Supreme Court judgment meant. The Tribunal observed that he had done exactly that himself when he decided the judgment applied only prospectively, and that he could not make his own reading the basis of a decision while denying the Tribunal jurisdiction to test it.

You get the principal and nothing else

Absa asked for interest from the date of the Supreme Court judgment. The Tribunal refused, finding nothing in the Act that empowers it to award interest on overpaid tax, and left open any entitlement arising from delay in paying the refund itself.

So Absa, having parted with Sh317.2 million in December 2020, recovers the same figure in 2027. The demand it answered had carried interest of Sh277 million, roughly 87 per cent of the principal. That asymmetry is worth pricing into any decision about whether to pay under protest or fight for a stay.

What it means

For anyone who accounted for withholding tax on card scheme fees or interchange for periods before 1 July 2026, the position is now clear, and the Commissioner’s own record is unhelpful to him. In the Supreme Court he submitted that the appellant and other banks had paid the assessed tax after the Court of Appeal judgment. The consents he signed in April 2026 concede the characterisation for later years.

Two cautions. The holding that the five year clock runs from the reversal rather than the payment is a first instance decision and may be appealed, so a protective claim is safer than reliance on the later start date. And the law has since moved. The Finance Act 2026 amended the definitions with effect from 1 July 2026 so that these payments are now caught expressly, which we wrote about when the same Act changed the VAT treatment of payment fees. The window this judgment opens is a window on the past.

The wider principle is not confined to card payments. Any taxpayer who has satisfied a demand founded on a judgment later overturned is in the same position, and the reasoning applies whatever the tax. Paying under an enforceable order protects you from enforcement. It does not concede the money.
